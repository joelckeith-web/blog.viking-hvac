# Mini-Split or Central Heat Pump? Choosing for a Phoenix Home

A mini-split and a central heat pump are both heat pumps, so either one can cool your home in summer and heat it in winter. The big difference is how the air reaches your rooms. A central heat pump sends conditioned air through ductwork to vents around the house. A ductless mini-split skips the ducts and delivers air straight into the room from an indoor unit mounted on a wall or ceiling.

Two questions can help you make the choice. What shape are your ducts in? And are you trying to heat and cool the whole house, or one part of it? Your answers may point toward one system, the other, or a mix of both.

## Both Are Heat Pumps, With One Big Difference

A central heat pump is the ducted kind. It uses an outdoor unit and an indoor unit called an air handler. You can think of a ducted heat pump as [a central air conditioner that can also run in reverse](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps) to heat the whole house in winter. In a ducted system, [one centrally located air handler blows conditioned air through a system of ducts](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps). Those ducts deliver the air to each room through registers, or vents, in the floor, ceiling or wall.

A ductless mini-split heats and cools without any of that ductwork. [Instead of ducts, it uses one or more head units](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ductless_heating_cooling) mounted on an interior wall or ceiling, paired with a unit outside. Refrigerant carries the heat directly to the heads inside. It travels through a line set, the [refrigerant tubing and control wiring that connect the indoor and outdoor units](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps).

A mini-split can serve one room or several. The simplest setup pairs a single outdoor unit with a single indoor unit. A [multi-split system](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps) connects several indoor heads to a single outdoor unit, so one outdoor unit can serve more than one space.

**Key Takeaway:** Both systems are heat pumps that heat and cool. A central heat pump sends air from one air handler through ducts to your rooms. A mini-split puts an indoor unit in each space it serves and needs no ductwork.

## Whole-House or Room-by-Room: How Comfort Differs

A central heat pump works from one air handler. It pushes conditioned air through the ducts to every room that has a vent. How well that works depends on the ducts, which the next section covers.

A mini-split works room by room. [Each indoor unit operates independently of the others](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps), so different areas of the home can be set to different temperatures if you want them to be. Mitsubishi Electric describes it this way in its [guide to zoned homes](https://www.mitsubishicomfort.com/articles/zoned-home): "You can make a single room a zone or you can establish a larger zone consisting of multiple rooms or an entire floor."

That room-by-room control has a limit. It often does not make sense to put an indoor unit in every single room, and [rooms without indoor units may become uncomfortable](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps). When you judge what one wall unit can do, [remember that heat rises and cool air sinks](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ductless_heating_cooling). Open spaces are generally easier to cool. Heat can have a hard time moving through an open doorway, and it can't move through a closed one. That is why the number and placement of indoor units matter so much with a mini-split.

**Key Takeaway:** With a mini-split, each indoor unit holds its own temperature. Rooms without an indoor unit may become uncomfortable. A central heat pump sends air from one air handler to every room on the duct system.

## Where Does Each System Fit Best?

### When a central heat pump makes sense

A ducted heat pump [can connect to the conventional forced-air ductwork](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps) that is typical of most American homes. If your home already has ducts and you are replacing an aging air conditioner or heat pump, a central heat pump can use that existing ductwork. It is the familiar way to heat and cool the whole house at once. Our [HVAC system replacement guide](https://blog.viking-hvac.com/blog/hvac-system-replacement-guide-chandler-phoenix-valley) covers the wider replacement decision.

The ducts have to be up to the job, though. ENERGY STAR's list of [signs of poorly performing ducts](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/duct-sealing) includes rooms that are difficult to heat and cool and stuffy rooms that never seem to feel comfortable. It also includes ducts located in an attic, crawlspace or the garage. If your ducts run through a hot Phoenix attic, have them looked at before you choose a system. Our [ductwork services](https://viking-hvac.com/residential/ductwork-services/) page explains the duct work we handle.

### When a mini-split makes sense

Mini-splits are [specifically designed for homes or sections of homes that do not have ductwork](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ductless_heating_cooling). They are increasingly being used in a garage, casita or addition where extending ductwork or heating and cooling capacity is difficult. They are also increasingly used in a room next to unconditioned space, such as a room over the garage, in rooms with hot or cold spots, and in older homes with no ductwork. Our post on [where ductless mini-splits fit in a Phoenix home](https://blog.viking-hvac.com/blog/ductless-mini-splits-phoenix) goes room by room.

**Key Takeaway:** A central heat pump fits a home with ducts in good condition when you want the whole house heated and cooled together. A mini-split fits spaces without ducts, such as a garage, casita or addition. Mini-splits are also increasingly used in rooms with hot or cold spots.

## Can You Combine Both in One Home?

Yes. A mini-split does not have to replace your central system. It can take care of a space that ducts don't reach well, such as an addition or a room with hot or cold spots. Meanwhile, the central heat pump keeps serving the rest of the house.

Mitsubishi Electric's zoning guidance describes whole-home setups where [ducted units commonly condition larger zones and ductless units condition smaller zones](https://www.mitsubishicomfort.com/articles/zoned-home). For those setups, the same guidance recommends keeping the ductwork compact and inside the insulated, heated and cooled part of the house.

Combining systems means planning which rooms each one will serve. That planning is part of sizing, which is the next step for any heat pump.

## Sizing and Installation Start With a Load Calculation

Size matters with either system. According to [ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ductless_heating_cooling), "Installing properly sized HVAC equipment for your home is essential to getting the best performance and comfort." Your contractor should verify the proper size using a tool called Manual J. It calculates how much heating and cooling your home needs to keep you warm in winter and cool in summer. [Manual J is ACCA's residential load calculation standard](https://www.acca.org/standards/technical-manuals/manual-j).

A mini-split adds a few more decisions. The load calculation is done for [each space the heat pump will condition](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/ductless-mini-split-heat-pumps). The installer then decides the number, type and location of the indoor units, or heads. Placement matters inside and out. [Running the line set through closets, attic stairways, attached garages or attics](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ductless_heating_cooling) can reduce the need to patch or paint walls and ceilings. Outside, give the unit unobstructed airflow by avoiding tight spots near shrubs or structures. Consider service access when you choose its spot, too.

You can read more about [how we approach HVAC installation](https://viking-hvac.com/residential/hvac-installation/).

**Key Takeaway:** Size either system with a Manual J load calculation. A mini-split also needs a plan for how many indoor units go where. A central heat pump depends on ducts in good condition.

## Keeping Either System Running Well

Filter care is much the same for both systems. [Heat pumps work best with clean filters](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps), and heat pump filters should be cleaned when visibly dirty or when the filter light tells you to. Check them regularly, especially after a dust storm.

It is also worth [considering regular maintenance of your heating and cooling system](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps) to help prevent future problems. For steps to take as the weather turns, see our post on [preparing your HVAC system for colder weather in the Phoenix Valley](https://blog.viking-hvac.com/blog/prepare-hvac-colder-weather-phoenix-valley).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a ductless mini-split a heat pump?

Yes. A ductless mini-split is a heat pump, so it both heats and cools. Its outdoor unit extracts heating or cooling from the outdoor air. Refrigerant carries the heat to one or more indoor units mounted on a wall or ceiling. A central heat pump works on the same idea, but it delivers the air through ducts instead of from indoor units in the rooms they serve.

### Does a central heat pump need ductwork?

Yes. A central heat pump is a ducted system. It can connect to the kind of forced-air ductwork that is typical of most American homes. One air handler blows conditioned air through the ducts to the vents in each room. If your home has no ducts, or the ducts are in poor shape, that should shape your choice. A mini-split does not need ductwork at all.

### Can one mini-split cool or heat my whole house?

A single indoor unit is installed inside the space it heats and cools. A multi-split system connects several indoor units to a single outdoor unit, so it can cover more of the house. Rooms without an indoor unit may become uncomfortable, though. Heat rises and cool air sinks. Heat can also have trouble moving through an open doorway, and it can't move through a closed one. That is why the number and placement of indoor units matter.

### Which system is better for heating and cooling the whole house?

A central heat pump is the familiar way to heat and cool the whole house at once, as long as the ducts are in good shape. One air handler sends air through the ducts to every room with a vent. A multi-split system can also cover more of the house, but each space it serves has its own indoor unit, and rooms without one may become uncomfortable.

### Can I add a mini-split to a home that already has a central heat pump?

Yes, the two can work in the same home. A mini-split can serve a space the ducts don't reach well, such as a room with hot or cold spots or a new addition. The central system keeps serving the rest of the house. Mitsubishi Electric describes whole-home setups in which ducted units commonly condition larger zones and ductless units condition smaller zones.

### How do I know what size system I need?

The right size comes from a load calculation. Your contractor should verify the proper size using Manual J. That calculation works out how much heating and cooling your home needs to keep you warm in winter and cool in summer. For a mini-split, the calculation covers each space the system will condition. The contractor also plans the number and location of indoor units.

### How can I tell if my ducts are good enough for a central heat pump?

Look for warning signs. ENERGY STAR lists these signs of poorly performing ducts: high summer and winter utility bills, rooms that are difficult to heat and cool, stuffy rooms that never seem to feel comfortable, ducts located in an attic, crawlspace or the garage, and tangled or kinked flexible ducts. If any of these sound familiar, have the ducts checked before you choose a system.

## Ducts, No Ducts, or Some of Each

A central heat pump and a mini-split both heat and cool, so the real choice is about your house. If your ducts are sound and you want every room handled together, a central heat pump is the natural fit. A garage, casita or addition that ducts can't easily reach, or a stubborn hot room, can point toward a mini-split. Some homes use both.
