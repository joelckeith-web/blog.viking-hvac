# Signs It's Time to Replace Your Phoenix-Area Furnace, and What Installation Involves

It may be time to replace a furnace when it keeps needing repairs or no longer heats your home evenly. Another reason is a safety problem that a technician says is not worth fixing. Age is part of the picture, but age alone does not decide it. A new furnace also has to be sized and installed correctly. Federal buying guidance says [an efficient furnace won't save energy or money if it isn't installed properly](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-furnaces).

## What Are the Signs Your Furnace Is Failing?

Because Valley winters are mild, a furnace can sit unused for months and then get asked to work hard on the first cold night. That makes it easy to miss slow changes. ENERGY STAR publishes a short list of [signs it may be time to replace heating and cooling equipment](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/replace). Most of them apply directly to a furnace.

### Repeat repairs and rising bills

The warning sign is frequent repairs, not a single one. When the same furnace needs a technician again and again, and your gas bill keeps climbing while you use it the same way, it's losing ground. Frequent repairs paired with rising energy bills are one of the signs on that list that it may be time to replace.

### Rooms that never feel right

Another warning sign is when some rooms in your home are too hot or too cold. Uneven heat does not always mean the furnace is the problem. [Federal purchasing guidance](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-furnaces) lists "poorly designed distribution systems, and leaky ducts" among installation problems that cause "occupant discomfort." A good contractor [will inspect your duct system](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/10-tips-hiring), if you have one, for air leaks and insulation and measure airflow. A proper diagnosis keeps you from replacing a furnace to solve a duct problem.

### New noises and an aging unit

A noisy system and an aging furnace are on the same list of signs to take seriously. A furnace that has started banging, rattling or squealing needs a look. Age by itself is a reason to plan ahead instead of waiting for a breakdown. It is not proof that the furnace has to go.

### A flame that doesn't look right

The burner flame tells a technician a lot about how the furnace is burning its fuel. A [crisp blue flame indicates efficient combustion](https://www.carrier.com/us/en/residential/hvac-resources/furnaces/furnace-inspection/), while a yellow or orange flame signals a dangerous mixture of gas and oxygen. Furnace maker Carrier says a comprehensive inspection involving gas lines, electrical testing, and heat exchanger analysis "must be performed by a licensed professional to ensure safety." If the flame looks wrong, have a licensed technician check it.

Every home with a gas furnace should also have working carbon monoxide alarms. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends that you ["Install battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas."](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center/Carbon-Monoxide-Questions-and-Answers) If an alarm sounds, the CPSC's instruction is: "Immediately move outside to fresh air. Call your emergency services, fire department, or 911." The CPSC adds: "After calling 911, do a head count to check that all persons are accounted for. DO NOT reenter the premises until the emergency services responders have given you permission."

**Key Takeaway:** Watch for a pattern of repeat repairs, rising bills, uneven rooms and new noises, and treat a yellow or orange burner flame as a call for a technician, not a do-it-yourself fix.

## Repair or Replace: Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Most furnace decisions do not come down to one sign. They come down to what failed, whether it is likely to fail again, and whether the rest of your system is healthy. It helps to know which questions to ask:

- **What exactly failed?** A worn part such as an igniter or blower motor is a different decision from a problem inside the heat exchanger.
- **Is this a safety part?** A [cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide](https://www.carrier.com/us/en/residential/hvac-resources/furnaces/furnace-inspection/), an odorless, invisible gas, into your home.
- **How often has the furnace needed repairs lately?** A pattern of repairs matters more than any single bill.
- **Does the furnace heat every room, or are the ducts part of the problem?**
- **Is the air conditioner getting older too?** If both halves of the system are aging, it can make sense to plan them together.

ENERGY STAR's [maintenance checklist](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/maintenance-checklist) explains why the burner and heat exchanger get so much attention: "A dirty burner or cracked heat exchanger causes improper burner operation. Either can cause the equipment to operate less safely and efficiently." A dirty burner can often be cleaned. A cracked heat exchanger is a much bigger conversation.

## Why Furnace Sizing Matters: Bigger Isn't Always Better

It is tempting to assume a bigger furnace heats better. ENERGY STAR is plain about it: ["A bigger system isn't always better; a contractor should size the heating and cooling system based on the size of your house, level of insulation, and windows."](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/10-tips-hiring)

The tool for that job is a load calculation. The Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) publishes [Manual J](https://www.acca.org/standards/technical-manuals/manual-j), the national standard for working out how much heating and cooling a home needs. In plain terms, a load calculation works out how much heating and cooling your specific house needs, based on how it is built. That is a better starting point than copying the size of the old furnace, which may never have been right in the first place.

Getting the size wrong costs you in comfort. [Federal buying guidance](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-furnaces) warns that installation mistakes cause efficiency losses, discomfort and a shorter equipment life. It names three: oversizing, poorly designed duct systems and leaky ducts.

**Key Takeaway:** Ask any installer whether they will do a load calculation for your home, and be cautious of a quote based only on the size of your old furnace.

## Standard or High-Efficiency Condensing Furnace?

Gas furnaces come in two broad types. A standard furnace sends its hot exhaust gases out through the vent. A high-efficiency condensing furnace uses a second step to pull more heat out of those gases before they leave. All ENERGY STAR certified furnaces are condensing furnaces, according to the [Federal Energy Management Program](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-furnaces). The program explains that "This technology takes advantage of normally exhausted heat in the furnace's flue gas to improve efficiency."

Condensing furnaces are built differently, and the installation reflects that. The same guidance says, "Condensing furnaces should not use indoor air for combustion," and recommends models with sealed combustion. Sealed combustion means the furnace brings outdoor air straight to the burners and keeps the combustion area sealed off from your living space. This design is safer because it prevents "exhaust gases from leaking or being back-drafted into occupied spaces." Condensing furnaces also "require slight modifications with installation," so the venting plan is part of the conversation.

Which type fits your home depends on your existing setup, your comfort goals and your budget. We'll lay out both options honestly so you can compare them side by side.

## What Happens on Furnace Installation Day

A good installation starts before anyone picks up a tool. ENERGY STAR says ["The contractor should spend significant time inspecting your current system and home to assess your needs."](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/10-tips-hiring) It also advises you to "Sign a written proposal with a contractor before work gets started." A written proposal protects you by spelling out costs, model numbers, the job schedule and warranty information.

On the day itself, the crew removes the old furnace and sets the new one in place. They connect it to your gas line, your ductwork, the electrical supply and the venting. Venting deserves real care. The Department of Energy says ["Combustion furnaces must be properly installed and vented to avoid backdrafting and the accumulation of pollutants."](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/hvac-combustion-furnaces) Your thermostat is connected, or replaced if the old one will not work well with the new equipment.

Before the crew leaves, ask them to start the furnace and check it while it runs, and to show you how the new system and thermostat work. Ask your installer before the job whether your city requires a permit and who will handle it. You can see the systems we install on our [HVAC installation page](https://viking-hvac.com/residential/hvac-installation/).

**Key Takeaway:** Expect a home evaluation and a written proposal before the job, and ask for the furnace to be started and checked, with a walkthrough, before the crew leaves.

## Should You Consider a Heat Pump or Dual-Fuel System Instead?

A furnace failing is a natural moment to ask whether a gas furnace is still the right choice. A heat pump heats your home in winter and cools it in summer. ENERGY STAR says that in most zones, [an air-source heat pump can be installed as a drop-in replacement](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps) when either your central AC or your furnace needs replacing. That option makes the most sense when your air conditioner is also getting older, since one system can replace both.

A dual-fuel system is a middle path. It pairs a heat pump with a gas furnace. Dual fuel systems ["allow for the flexibility of heating with a heat pump or with a more traditional gas or oil furnace."](https://www.energystar.gov/products/air_source_heat_pumps)

There is no single right answer. The best choice depends on the age of your air conditioner, your ductwork, whether you have gas service, and how you like your home to feel.
